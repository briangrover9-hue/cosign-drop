Everyone's a 4.8

AI now writes the resumes and AI reads them, so hiring leans harder on who will vouch for you. But every trust signal people have built for that job drifts to the top of its scale until it can no longer tell anyone apart.

Praise drifts because it costs nothing to give and something to withhold. The one fix that works on its own is tying praise to a specific piece of real work.

By Brian Grover @briantgrover Sources and method

01 · Why now

When writing got cheap, reputation got expensive

Freelancer.com ran the experiment before anyone planned it. In April 2023 the site gave workers on paid plans a tool that writes a proposal with one click. Economists Anaïs Galdin and Jesse Silbert then estimated how much more an employer would pay a worker for a proposal tailored to the job, and for a stronger track record on the site.

Once anyone could produce a tailored proposal, employers stopped paying much for one, and what they would pay for a strong track record rose by about two thirds. When the writing stops telling an employer anything, the employer leans on what past clients have said about the worker.

Everyone has the same tool now. In a 2025 survey of 1,421 job seekers, 29.3 percent had used AI on a resume or cover letter, up from 17.3 percent a year earlier. Employers are automating the other side: 43 percent of HR professionals told SHRM they use AI in their work, up from 26 percent the year before. So it matters what happens to vouches over time.

02 · The drift

Where ratings actually sit

Start with a scale everyone has used. Before you look, make a guess.

The pattern is old, and it shows up everywhere people rate each other. Here is each system drawn the same way: a hundred stars, with gold for the share at the top of its scale.

Where anyone kept records over time, the gold spreads. On one large online labor market, the average rating rose from 3.74 in 2007 to 4.85 in 2016. The two systems that never had a bottom show what happens when giving costs nothing. LinkedIn's one-click endorsements passed a billion in under six months. On GitHub, researchers found 6.0 million suspected fake stars, and in July 2024, 16.66 percent of repositories that gained 50 or more stars that month had run a fake-star campaign.

03 · Why it happens

Four ordinary forces push every scale up

None of this needs anyone to lie. Each force is small and reasonable on its own, and they all push the same way.

  1. Giving is free

    A one-click endorsement costs nothing, so people give them to be nice, to return a favor or to clear a notification. Signals that cost something behave differently. At two events run by a South Korean dating company, each participant got two or eight virtual roses to attach to date requests. All else equal, attaching a rose raised the chance a request was accepted by 3.3 percentage points, about a fifth. Economics job candidates can send only two signals of interest, and a signal came with a 6.8 point higher chance of an interview.

  2. The other person sees what you said

    When the person you rate can see your rating and rate you back, a low score invites a low score in return. On eBay, sellers answered negative feedback with negative feedback of their own, which kept buyers from leaving it in the first place. When Airbnb hid each review until both sides had written one, the correlation between guest and host ratings fell by 48 percent, though the average guest rating moved only 0.25 percent.

  3. Honesty costs you

    A bad rating can cost someone income, and most people would rather not be the reason. One large online labor market asked employers for a public star rating and a private score the worker would never see. Over the same period, private scores fell while public ratings of the same jobs rose. Of employers who said privately they would definitely not hire a worker again, 28.4 percent gave that worker four stars or more in public.

  4. Unhappy people go quiet

    Satisfied customers tend to leave feedback, and mildly unhappy ones mostly say nothing. On eBay, just 0.55 percent of buyers' comments were negative, yet one estimate puts the share of buyers who were actually dissatisfied at 18.5 to 21 percent. Count every sale, including the ones that got no feedback, and the average seller's positive share falls from 99.3 percent to 64 percent.

Then the feed makes it worse. On a social news site, one randomly assigned up-vote made the next viewer 32 percent more likely to up-vote, and those comments ended with ratings 25 percent higher. In an online music market of 14,341 people, showing download counts made the hits bigger and harder to predict. The sociologist Robert Merton called this the Matthew effect: recognition keeps flowing to the people who already have it.

04 · What predicts the job

Watching someone work beats counting their years

If vouches are going to carry more of hiring, it helps to know what predicts job performance in the first place. In 1998, Frank Schmidt and John Hunter pooled 85 years of research into one ranking of hiring methods. In 2022, Paul Sackett and colleagues redid the statistical corrections behind those numbers and found most of them had been set too high.

Work samples, where a candidate does a piece of the actual job, fell from .54 to .33, and three methods now rank above them. They still beat years of experience, which fell to .07, by a wide margin. Reference checks (.26) and years of education (.10) could not be re-estimated, because the studies behind them reported too little to check.

Two cautions. Work samples show one of the larger gaps between Black and white candidates' average scores in the same table, so how they are designed and scored matters. And coworkers rating each other scored .49 in 1998, but nobody has been able to re-check that number.

05 · The trust lab

Watch a team's vouches drift, then fix them

Here are 80 coworkers in ten teams. Each has a true skill you can't see. Every round, each person vouches for a few others on a scale of 1 to 5, and a person's score is the average of the last 12 vouches they received. The lab starts in the worst setting. Press play and watch what happens.

The trust lab needs JavaScript. In the model's worst setting, the average score climbs to about 4.7 and a top 10 by score includes about 2 of the 10 most skilled people. With every vouch tied to a piece of work, blind until both sides have written, limited to people who worked together and shown in a feed that ranks no one, the average settles near 3.2 and the top 10 includes about 8 of the 10.

Now flip the switches one at a time. Tying each vouch to a specific piece of work is the one fix that works on its own: the scores spread out again, and the top 10 by score starts to match the 10 most skilled. It has a cost. A vouch that takes real work gets given less often, so more people end up with no vouches at all. The other three switches do most of their good in combination, and with all four fixed, nobody goes unseen.

The lab is a model. Its rules come from the research above, and the sizes of its effects are our assumptions, which you can change under the lab. The methods list every rule and setting.

06 · What holds up

Praise tied to real work keeps its value

I spent nine months designing a hiring network built on vouches and work samples. The design came down to four rules, and each one answers a force from section 3.

  1. A vouch stays hidden until both people have written theirs

    Dana, about Omar Written. Hidden until Omar writes his.
    Dana, about Omar
    Omar rebuilt our billing retries in three weeks. Failed charges dropped, and I stopped getting paged on weekends.
    Omar, about Dana Written. Hidden until Dana writes hers.
    Omar, about Dana
    Dana ran the launch checklist and caught the tax bug two days before we shipped. I'd want her on the next launch.
    An example. Neither person could read the other's before writing.

    Neither card can be written to match the other, so there is nothing to trade. On Airbnb, blind reviews cut the correlation between the two sides' ratings by about half, and in a lab version of eBay's feedback system, blind ratings cut it from 0.68 to 0.41.

  2. It names something specific the person did

    A specific claim anchors the rating to the work instead of to what everyone else is getting, and that anchor is what stops the drift in the lab. It is also why work samples beat years of experience: they judge what someone actually did.

  3. It only comes from people who worked with you

    Expedia only lets guests who booked a stay review a hotel, while TripAdvisor lets anyone. Comparing the same hotels on both sites, economists found the signs of fake reviews on TripAdvisor were strongest exactly where hotels had the most reason to fake them. LinkedIn reached a similar conclusion in 2016, when it began highlighting endorsements from colleagues and from people who know the skill.

  4. It costs the giver something

    Scarce signals keep their meaning, as the roses and job-market signals in section 3 show. The simplest cost is your name. When the vouches you give sit on your own record, being wrong about someone costs you, and people get careful.

All four rules point the same way. Praise holds its value when it is attached to a real piece of work that someone else can look at. The work is the anchor, and the vouch says who saw it happen.

Cosign, which launched on September 25, gets the hardest part right. Every cosign carries a name and a reputation, and its guide asks people to be specific about what they know, such as "we built our first product together," instead of offering a generic compliment. The open question is whether a cosign could attach to the work itself, so the praise and the proof travel together.

07 · Methods and caveats

How this was put together

The Airbnb numbers

Inside Airbnb publishes snapshots of public Airbnb listings under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license. We used the detailed listings files for San Francisco and New York City, both compiled on 14 June 2026, and kept listings with a guest rating and at least 10 reviews: 3,592 in San Francisco and 12,102 in New York. The rating is the overall score Airbnb shows on each listing. Their median is 4.84 and their mean 4.79, which gives the title its number.

Counting every rated listing (27,448), the median is 4.88 and 62.8 percent sit at 4.8 or above. We use the 10-review cut because a single five-star review can give a new listing a perfect score. Inside Airbnb no longer serves its older snapshots, so we make no claim about how these ratings changed over time. The script that computes every Airbnb number here is research/airbnb_measure.py.

Why now

Their Table 2 converts how employers weigh each part of an application into dollars: the extra bid an employer would accept for one standard deviation more of it. We quote the columns limited to applications the employer considered. The job seeker figures come from iHire's State of Online Recruiting 2025, a survey by a job site, and the HR figures from SHRM's 2025 Talent Trends survey of 2,040 HR professionals.

Job performance

The 1998 values are Schmidt and Hunter's operational validities for overall job performance. Five of them, including work samples, reference checks and peer ratings, were corrected only for measurement error because range restriction could not be estimated. The 2022 values come from Sackett and colleagues' Table 3, which drops the range-restriction corrections they judged too large. They did not re-estimate eight of the 1998 values, including reference checks, peer ratings and years of education. In a 2023 follow-up they lowered cognitive ability again, to .23, using an unpublished analysis. Their method is disputed: see Oh, Le and Roth (2023) and the reply that followed. The same 2022 table gives work samples a Black-white difference of d = .67, one of the larger gaps among the methods listed.

The trust lab

The lab is a simulation, written for this page, of how scores evolve under simple rules. It does not use real people or real data. Its code is in js/lab-model.js.

  • People. 80 coworkers in ten teams of eight. Each has a hidden skill drawn from a bell curve and a visibility (how much of a presence they have) that is only loosely related to skill. Each person has also worked with at least two people on other teams.
  • Rounds. Each of 30 rounds, every person vouches for a few others: three one-tap vouches, or one written vouch, or one vouch tied to a piece of work. A score is the average of the last 12 vouches a person received.
  • Judgment. Coworkers judge your skill with some noise. People who never worked with you judge mostly your visibility. A vouch tied to work is judged from the work itself, which reflects skill with less noise.
  • The drift. Raters judge people relative to the current average rating, then add a little on top, because a below-average rating feels like a penalty. With no fixed standard, that extra compounds every round. A written vouch is judged 30 percent against a fixed standard, and a vouch tied to work 75 percent, which is what stops the compounding.
  • Reflexive fives. Forty percent of one-tap vouches, and ten percent of written ones, are fives given without judging.
  • Visible or blind. A visible vouch is pulled 40 percent toward whatever the other person said about you, and shades up more (0.12 stars) than a blind one (0.05).
  • Feed. A feed ranked by followers shows people more often the more followers they have, each vouch they receive adds followers, and follower counts sway judgment.
  • Measures. "At 4.8 or above" counts people whose score is at least 4.8. "Top 10 by score" counts how many of the 10 highest scores belong to the 10 most skilled people; chance alone gives about one. People with no vouches rank last.

The rules follow the research in sections 3 and 6, but every number in them is our assumption. Change them under the lab and the sizes of the effects change with them. What holds across the settings we tried is the direction: scores judged only against each other drift up, and scores anchored to the work do not.

Numbers that didn't survive

These hiring statistics get repeated widely. Each fell apart when we traced it to its source.

  • "46 percent of resumes contain false information." It comes from a 2017 OfficeTeam survey in which 46 percent of workers said they knew someone who had lied on a resume. That measures what people have heard about others, and it is often credited to SHRM.
  • "Only 0.4 percent of applications succeed." A blog divided one hire by an average of 242 applications. No study measured it.
  • "Referrals are 7 percent of applicants and 40 percent of hires." It is usually credited to Jobvite, and we could not find it in any Jobvite report we read.
  • "63 percent of candidates get ghosted." We could not match it to any study. A related figure is real: in a 2023 Indeed survey, 62 percent of job seekers said they planned to ghost an employer.
