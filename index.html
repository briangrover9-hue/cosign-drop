Everyone’s a 4.8

AI now writes the resumes and AI reads them, so hiring leans harder on who will vouch for you. That’s what professional networks run on, from LinkedIn endorsements to Cosign’s cosigns. But the trust signals people have built for that job tend to drift toward the top of their scales, where they tell fewer and fewer people apart.

Praise drifts because it costs nothing to give and something to withhold. Once every score sits near the top, the signal moves to who gave the praise and what they said.

By Brian Grover @briantgrover Sources and method

01 · Why now

When writing got cheap, reputation got expensive

Freelancer.com ran the experiment before anyone planned it. ChatGPT arrived in November 2022, and in April 2023 the site gave workers on paid plans a tool that writes a proposal with one click. Economists Anaïs Galdin and Jesse Silbert compared job posts from before ChatGPT with job posts from 2024. They estimated how much more an employer would pay for a proposal tailored to the job, and for a worker with a higher reputation score, which the site computes mostly from past performance there.

Once anyone could produce a tailored proposal, employers would pay about 42 percent less for one, and what they would pay for a higher reputation score rose by about two thirds. When the writing tells an employer less, the employer leans harder on the worker’s record.

Everyone has the same tool now. In a 2025 survey of 1,421 job seekers, 29.3 percent had used AI on a resume or cover letter, up from 17.3 percent a year earlier. Employers are adopting it too: in SHRM’s 2025 survey of HR professionals, 43 percent of organizations used AI for HR tasks, up from 26 percent in 2024. So it matters what happens to vouches over time.

02 · The drift

Where ratings actually sit

Start with a scale everyone has used. Before you look, make a guess.

The pattern is old, and it shows up in system after system where people rate each other. Here is each one drawn the same way: a hundred stars, with gold for the share at the top of its scale.

Where anyone kept records over time, the gold spreads. On one large online labor market, the average rating rose from 3.74 in 2007 to 4.85 in 2016. TripAdvisor hotels are the exception: guests there rate a business that does not rate them back, and only 26 percent of hotels sit at 4.5 or above. The researchers who measured it suggest people rate other people more tactfully than they rate firms. The two systems that never had a bottom show what happens when giving costs nothing. LinkedIn’s one-click endorsements passed a billion in under six months. On GitHub, researchers found 6.0 million suspected fake stars, and in July 2024, 16.66 percent of repositories that gained 50 or more stars that month had run a fake-star campaign.

A count of yeses saturates the same way. Once everyone has plenty, the number says little, and the signal lives in who said yes and why. Cosign’s own guide makes the point: “Read who is saying what, and why.”

03 · The trust lab

Watch vouches drift, then change the rules

It’s the same problem any network of endorsements has to solve. Here are 80 coworkers at one company, in ten teams of eight, each with a true skill you can’t see. Every round, each person gives three one-tap vouches or one longer one, and each vouch is as likely to go to someone they worked with as to whoever the feed shows them. The lab starts in the worst setting and plays by itself once it’s on screen, or when you press play if your device is set to reduce motion.

The trust lab needs JavaScript. In the model’s worst setting, the average score climbs from about 3.5 to 4.4, and a top 10 by score includes 4 or 5 of the 10 most skilled people. Tying each vouch to a piece of work keeps the average near 3.3 and lifts that to about 6. Chance alone would put about 1 of the 10 in the top 10.

Flip the switches one at a time, or start from the Cosign-style setting: a named, public yes with a reason, saying how the giver knows you, in a feed ranked by the track record of who said yes. Across a dozen simulated companies, those settings find about 6 of the 10 most skilled when the feed ranks by track record and about 4 when it ranks by raw count; ranking no one does about as well as ranking by track record. One-tap yeses in a feed ranked by count, roughly how LinkedIn endorsements work, find about 2, and people say yes about 78 percent of the time. Chance alone finds about 1.

Across those companies, tying each vouch to a piece of work does the most on its own, and we should be plain about why. It is our most generous setting: we assume a vouch that points at work has no reflexive fives or yeses, reads skill with less noise, is judged on the work even by strangers, and is held to a fixed standard. That standard, the slider next to the switches, is what keeps the scores from drifting. With the other switches at their worst, lower it and the scores climb nearly as much as one-tap stars do. Weaken the other assumptions in the drawer and the gap between tied-to-work and the rest narrows, and at the extremes it closes.

On the yes scale, a yes that takes effort is rarer, and a ranked feed sends strangers’ vouches to the people it already shows, so together they leave about a quarter of the company with no yes at all; with a feed that ranks no one, about 5 of the 80 go without. Silence there can mean nobody looked, and Cosign’s guide says as much: “Having few or no cosigns is not a negative judgment about a person.” The lab is a model: its rules come from the research in the next section, the sizes of its effects are our assumptions, and the methods list every rule.

04 · Why it happens

Four ordinary forces push every scale up

None of this needs anyone to lie. Each force is small and reasonable on its own, and they all push the same way.

  1. 1. Giving is free

    A one-click endorsement costs nothing to give, so little holds anyone back from giving one. Scarce signals behave differently. At two events run by a South Korean dating company, each participant got two or eight virtual roses to attach to date requests, free to send but limited in number. All else equal, attaching a rose raised the chance a request was accepted by 3.3 percentage points, about a fifth. Economics job candidates can send only two signals of interest, and a signal came with a 6.8 point higher chance of an interview.

  2. 2. The other person sees what you said

    When the person you rate can see your rating and rate you back, a low score invites a low score in return. On eBay, sellers answered negative feedback with negative feedback of their own, which kept buyers from leaving it in the first place. When Airbnb hid each review until both sides had written one, the correlation between guest and host ratings fell by 48 percent, though the average guest rating moved only 0.25 percent.

  3. 3. Honesty costs you

    A bad rating can cost someone income, and most people would rather not be the reason. One large online labor market asked employers for a public star rating and a private score the worker would never see. Over the same period, private scores fell while public ratings of the same jobs rose. Of employers who said privately they would definitely not hire a worker again, 28.4 percent gave that worker four stars or more in public.

  4. 4. Unhappy people go quiet

    Satisfied customers tend to leave feedback, and mildly unhappy ones mostly say nothing. On eBay, just 0.55 percent of buyers’ comments were negative, yet one estimate puts the share of buyers who were actually dissatisfied at 18.5 to 21 percent. Count every sale, including the ones that got no feedback, and the average seller’s positive share falls from 99.3 percent to 64 percent.

Then the feed makes it worse. On a social news site, one randomly assigned up-vote made the next viewer 32 percent more likely to up-vote, and those comments ended with ratings 25 percent higher. In an online music market of 14,341 people, showing download counts made the hits bigger and harder to predict. The sociologist Robert Merton called this the Matthew effect: recognition keeps flowing to the people who already have it.

05 · What predicts the job

Watching someone work beats counting their years

Work samples, where a candidate does a piece of the actual job, predict performance better than years of experience, reference checks or education. A 2022 re-analysis cut their score from .54 to .33 and ranked three methods above them, but years of experience fell further, to .07.

06 · What holds up

Praise keeps its value when you can see what it rests on

The research points to four rules for a vouch that keeps its value, and Cosign already ships a version of most of them.

  1. A vouch stays hidden until both people have written theirs

    Dana, about Omar Written. Hidden until Omar writes his.
    Dana, about Omar
    Omar rebuilt our billing retries in three weeks. Failed charges dropped, and I stopped getting paged on weekends.
    Omar, about Dana Written. Hidden until Dana writes hers.
    Omar, about Dana
    Dana ran the launch checklist and caught the tax bug two days before we shipped. I’d want her on the next launch.
    An example. Neither person could read the other’s before writing.

    Neither card can be written to match the other, so there is nothing to trade. On Airbnb, blind reviews cut the correlation between the two sides’ ratings by about half, and in a lab version of eBay’s feedback system, blind ratings cut it from 0.68 to 0.41. Cosign’s private signals work on a related idea: the recipient sees the kind of signal, but it isn’t public, and when the interest is reciprocal, Cosign can open a private conversation.

  2. It names something specific the person did

    A specific claim anchors praise to the work instead of to what everyone else is getting. Hiring research points the same way: watching someone do the work predicts performance better than counting their years. Cosign’s guide pushes for this, asking for “a specific contribution” over “a generic compliment.”

  3. It says how you know them

    Expedia only lets guests who booked a stay review a hotel, while TripAdvisor lets anyone. Comparing the same hotels, economists found the signs of fake reviews on TripAdvisor were strongest where hotels had the most reason to fake them, and LinkedIn began highlighting endorsements from colleagues in 2016. Cosign asks cosigners to be specific about what they know and not to “imply a relationship or experience you haven’t had,” and a private “Work/Worked Together” signal lets people mark an existing or past relationship.

  4. It costs the giver something

    Scarce signals keep their meaning, as the roses and job-market signals in section 4 show. The simplest cost is your name. Cosign describes a cosign as putting “your name and reputation behind that belief,” and the cosigns you give become “a useful record of the people you’ve believed in.”

One more finding applies to every network that ranks people. A feed that ranks by raw count hands the gains to whoever is already ahead. On Cosign’s ranked lists, ordering “can reflect the reputation behind contributions as well as how many people have contributed,” and in the lab, ranking by the track record of who said yes finds more of the most skilled people than ranking by count, about 6 of 10 against about 4.

All four rules point the same way: praise holds its value when a reader can see who gave it and what it rests on. The question none of this settles is how much a vouch gains from being tied to a specific piece of work. Our lab says a lot, but only because of settings we chose; answering it would take real vouches and real outcomes.

07 · Methods and caveats

How this was put together

The Airbnb numbers

Inside Airbnb publishes snapshots of public Airbnb listings under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license. We used the detailed listings files for San Francisco and New York City, both compiled on 14 June 2026, and kept listings with a guest rating and at least 10 reviews: 3,592 in San Francisco and 12,102 in New York. The rating is the overall score Airbnb shows on each listing. Their median is 4.84 and their mean 4.79, which gives the title its number.

Counting every rated listing (27,448), the median is 4.88 and 62.8 percent sit at 4.8 or above. We use the 10-review cut because a single five-star review can give a new listing a perfect score. Inside Airbnb no longer serves its older snapshots, so we make no claim about how these ratings changed over time. The script that computes every Airbnb number here is research/airbnb_measure.py.

Why now

Galdin and Silbert (2025) use Freelancer.com job posts from January 2021 to July 2024. Their Table 2 compares posts from before ChatGPT’s release on November 30, 2022 with posts from March 26, 2024 on, by which time the site had its own AI writing tool (from April 2023). It turns how much employers favor each part of an application into dollars: how much higher a bid an employer would accept for one step up, one standard deviation, in that part. We quote the columns limited to applications the employer looked at. The reputation score is the site’s own ranking of workers, built mostly from their record there; employers see the ranking and most of what goes into it, but not the score itself. The job seeker figures come from iHire’s State of Online Recruiting 2025, a survey by a job site, and the HR figures from SHRM’s 2025 Talent Trends, a February 2025 survey of 2,040 HR professionals about their organizations.

Job performance

The numbers are correlations between each method’s score and later job performance, after statistical corrections. Schmidt and Hunter corrected for imperfect measures of performance and for the fact that studies only see people who were hired, a narrower group than everyone who applied. Sackett and colleagues found that second correction had been made too large for most methods, which is why most of their 2022 values (their Table 3) are lower. Work samples fell for a different reason: they replaced an estimate from before meta-analysis existed with a newer meta-analysis of 54 studies. Five of the 1998 values, including work samples, reference checks and peer ratings, never got that second correction, and eight could not be re-estimated in 2022, including reference checks, peer ratings (coworkers rating each other, at .49) and years of education. In a 2023 follow-up the same authors lowered cognitive ability again, to .23, using an unpublished analysis, and their method is disputed: see Oh, Le and Roth (2023) and the reply that followed. One more caution: in the 2022 table, Black candidates’ average work-sample scores sit about two thirds of a standard deviation below white candidates’, one of the larger gaps among the methods listed, so how work samples are designed and scored matters.

The trust lab

The lab is a simulation, written for this page, of how scores evolve under simple rules. It does not use real people or real data. Its code is in js/lab-model.js.

  • People. 80 coworkers in ten teams of eight. Each has a hidden skill drawn from a bell curve and a visibility (how much of a presence they have) that is only loosely related to skill. Each person also picks two people at random on other teams as people they have worked with, rarely the same one twice, and those links go both ways.
  • Rounds. Each of 30 rounds, every person gives three one-tap vouches, or one written vouch, or one vouch tied to a piece of work. Each vouch is as likely to go to someone they worked with as to whoever the feed shows them.
  • Judgment. Coworkers judge your skill with some noise. People who never worked with you judge mostly your visibility. A vouch tied to work is judged from the work, which shows skill with less noise, even to strangers.
  • Stars. Raters judge people partly against the current average rating and add a little on top, because a below-average rating feels like a penalty; with no fixed standard, that extra compounds every round. A written vouch is judged 30 percent against a fixed standard and a vouch tied to work 75 percent, the slider next to the switches. Twelve percent of one-tap ratings and four percent of written ones are reflexive fives. A score is the average of the last 12 vouches a person received.
  • A named yes. There is no way to say no, only silence. A giver says yes when someone seems at least average (one tap) or clearly above average (written or tied to work), and a quarter of one-tap judgments, and 3 percent of written ones, are a yes given without judging. A person’s standing is the number of yeses they have received, weighted as the next two rules describe.
  • Visible or blind. A visible star rating is pulled 40 percent toward what the other person last gave the rater, and shades up more (0.12 stars) than a blind one (0.05). A visible yes makes a yes back easier.
  • How they know you. When a vouch says how the giver knows you, a vouch from someone who never worked with you counts a quarter as much.
  • Feed. A feed ranked by count shows people more often the more vouches they have, and the counts sway judgment. A feed ranked by who vouched weights each vouch by the giver’s track record, meaning how the people they vouched for (with a yes, or four or five stars) have turned out, which the model reveals a little each round with noise; that weighting sets the scores too. In either ranked feed, a person’s place in it sways how others judge them, by the same amount for every kind of vouch. A plain feed ranks no one.
  • Measures. “At 4.8 or above” counts people whose score is at least 4.8. “Top 10 by score” counts how many of the 10 highest scores or standings belong to the 10 most skilled people; chance alone gives about one. Ties are broken at random, once per run, and people with no vouches rank last. The page shows one simulated company, picked from 600 because its first run sits closest to the average of a dozen and ranks the single switches in the same order; the numbers in the text are those averages.

The rules follow the research in sections 4 and 6, but every number in them is our assumption, including the ones that make tying vouches to work look strong. Change them under the lab and the sizes of the effects change with them. What held across the settings we tried is the direction: scores judged only against each other drift up, scores anchored to the work do not, and on the yes scale, weighting who said yes beats counting yeses.

Numbers that didn’t survive

These hiring statistics get repeated widely. Each fell apart when we traced it to its source.

  • “46 percent of resumes contain false information.” It comes from a 2017 OfficeTeam survey in which 46 percent of workers said they knew someone who had lied on a resume. That measures what people have heard about others, and it is often credited to SHRM.
  • “Only 0.4 percent of applications succeed.” A blog divided one hire by an average of 242 applications. No study measured it.
  • “Referrals are 7 percent of applicants and 40 percent of hires.” It is usually credited to Jobvite, and we could not find it in any Jobvite report we read.
  • “63 percent of candidates get ghosted.” We could not match it to any study. A related figure is real: in a 2023 Indeed survey, 62 percent of job seekers said they planned to ghost an employer.
