Everyone’s a 4.8

We rate everything now: drivers, restaurants, apps, coworkers. Approval got cheap, and cheap approval turns into a commodity that can’t tell anyone apart. Here’s how it happens, and how to stop it.

By Brian Grover · @briantgrover · Methods and sources

It’s the same everywhere

Grades, eBay, LinkedIn, GitHub. Wherever people rate each other, the scores crowd the top. Even a simple yes, like an endorsement or a referral, piles up until the count says little.

Each grid is 100 stars. Gold is the share at the top of each scale. Hotels, rated as businesses, are the exception. All sources ·

Why ratings drift

Praise is free: an endorsement takes one click. People see what you said, so a low score invites one back; when Airbnb hid reviews until both sides had written one, the link between guest and host ratings fell by about half. Unhappy people stay quiet: on eBay, fewer than 1 in 100 comments was negative, though one estimate puts unhappy buyers at about 1 in 5. And people go easier on people: hotels, which can’t rate guests back, drift far less, and the researchers suggest people rate “other individuals differently or more tactfully, than they rate firms.”

  • Harvard grades that are A’s: 24 percent in 2005, 60.2 percent in 2025.
  • eBay feedback that is positive, for the average seller: 99.3 percent.
  • LinkedIn endorsements and GitHub stars can only say yes: 100 percent.
  • Airbnb listings rated 4.5 or higher in 2015: 94 percent. TripAdvisor hotels: 26 percent.

How the lab works

It’s a simulation written for this page: 80 coworkers, each with a hidden skill level, rate each other for 30 rounds. Half the ratings go to people they worked with and half to whoever a feed shows, and the feed favors whoever has the most. Each one-tap rating is judged against the current average, plus a little, so with nothing fixed to judge against, the scores climb.

Why real work is hard to fake

A vouch that points at real work can be checked against the work, so it says more about skill than about who is well known. Hiring research finds the same thing: a work sample, where a candidate does a piece of the real job, predicts job performance better than years of experience do (Sackett and colleagues, 2022). In the lab, how firmly real work anchors a rating is our assumption, and “Try other rules” lets you change it.

How this works

  1. 80 pretend coworkers. Each one is secretly good or not so good at their job.
  2. Every round, they rate each other. Some ratings are honest, some are just being nice.
  3. We check: do the highest-rated people turn out to be the best ones? Black dots are the best. Gold stars are the highest rated.

This lab needs JavaScript. In the simulation, with one-click ratings that everyone can see, the scores drift up toward 5, and the top 10 by score holds only about half of the 10 most skilled people. When every rating points at real work, the scores stay spread out, and the top 10 holds most of them.

What this means for a cosign

Most ratings, referrals and endorsements count every vote the same. People don’t. A yes from someone whose judgment you trust, written with care, is worth more than a pile of taps.

That’s how you keep approval from turning into a commodity, and it’s what Cosign is built around: named yeses, context from people who have worked together, and who said yes and why. The next step is tying each yes to the work itself.

What Cosign already does

A cosign puts “your name and reputation behind that belief,” and Cosign promises “attributable context from people who have worked together.” Its guide asks for “a specific contribution, a moment you saw someone’s judgment at work, or a clear account of their strengths” over “a generic compliment,” and on ranked lists, ordering “can reflect the reputation behind contributions as well as how many people have contributed.” Its advice to readers: “Read who is saying what, and why.”
